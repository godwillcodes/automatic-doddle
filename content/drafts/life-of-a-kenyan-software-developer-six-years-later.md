*Part two. [Part one](https://iamgodwillb.medium.com/life-of-a-kenyan-software-developer-abafacee6b18) was published in September 2020.*

Six years ago, almost to the day, I wrote down a day in my life as a 23 year old developer in Nairobi. Up at five for the gym. A 60 bob matatu into town. Codecademy until seven. Asleep by eleven, watching cartoons. People still send me that post.

I'm 28 now. I wrote the same day again to see what changed.

## 3:00 AM

At 23 the day started at five, with a jog to the gym.

Now it starts at three, and I'm working on [SpaceYako](https://spaceyako.com).

SpaceYako is a property platform for Kenya, and the whole product is built around one question: can you believe the listing? Anyone who has looked for a house here knows why that question needs a company.

Six years ago I would have written this hour up as discipline. It isn't. I'm awake because I can't sleep, and the work is whatever happens to be open on the screen.

Some of the best code I wrote this year happened at 3am. So did some of the worst decisions, on the same laptop, twenty minutes apart, and at the time I couldn't have told you which was which.

I've also spent a lot of this year talking to AI at 3am. It is always awake. It never gets tired of you. It has nowhere else to be and nobody to get back to, which makes it exactly the wrong company at that hour, because the one thing it won't do unless you ask is tell you to go to bed.

> Something that agrees with you fluently at 3am is not your friend.

## 6:30 AM

In part one, breakfast was almost always an egg, bread and tea, and I packed my own meals and reheated them in the company kitchen.

Now there's a chef who plans my meals for the week. I live in an apartment in Kiambu County that the 23 year old paying 15,000 shillings a month in Roysambu could not have pictured.

For most of this month I haven't eaten the food.

That's the part I want on the record, more than the apartment. You can assemble every piece of a good life, pay for it, have it delivered on a schedule, and still not be well enough to use any of it.

> Comfort is not care. I had built one and assumed it was the other.

## 7:20 AM

Six years ago, 7:20 was a matatu into town: mood music, and *To Kill a Mockingbird* open on my lap. The fare was 60 bob. An Uber was 500. I wrote both numbers down, because both mattered.

Now it's the car.

I wanted a car my whole life. Not a particular car, just the fact of one: walking out of a building, getting into something that was mine, and going where I wanted without asking anyone or counting anything.

I bought it three years ago. A 2007 VW Golf Mk5.

It's nineteen years old and it was never a status object. It's the unglamorous, achievable version of something I had wanted since I was a boy, and the first morning I drove it to work is still one of the best mornings of my life.

Getting the thing you wanted your whole life doesn't change what it is, though. It's a car. It sits in the same traffic the matatu sat in. It changed my life in every practical way, and it has never once made a bad month easier.

At 23 I thought the car was a destination. At 28 I know it was a tool.

## 8:15 AM

Read part one back and you notice that every number in it is a price. Rent, 15,000. Fare, 60. Uber, 500. Every figure in that post is money leaving.

At 23 that was my whole financial worldview. Money arrives, money goes, and the only interesting question is where.

This year that flipped, and I know exactly what did it. I worked out that I now earn more than both my parents ever did, combined.

It wasn't the triumph I expected. It was closer to vertigo. If I'm the highest earner my family has produced, there's nobody above me to ask and no template to follow. Everything I know about money I learned from people who never had the problem I have now.

Nobody teaches you the second question. Everything around you is built to keep you answering the first one. So this year, for the first time, I stopped asking where the money goes and started asking how much of it I keep.

## 9:00 AM

In part one, nine o'clock was scrum with the designers and project managers, on React, GraphQL, Gatsby and a headless WordPress. Now it's Next.js, TypeScript, Drizzle, and a quantity of WordPress I don't advertise.

Today isn't a code day, though. Today is samples.

We're making merchandise for political clients, and the first round came back. The dark green polos are good as blanks. Then the embroidery goes on and they fall apart: the fabric is too light for the stitch density, the logo pulls the knit, and you can feel the stitching through the shirt when you wear it. It reads rough instead of clean.

I gave it a 6.5 and asked for the heavier weight.

It's a strange thing to be completely sharp about how a polo shirt carries embroidery at nine in the morning, on no sleep, and unable to remember whether you brushed your teeth. I would never sign off a 6.5 for a client. I hold myself to nothing close to that, and nobody has ever asked me to.

That's the only honest answer I have to how you work through a year like this. You don't. Some parts of you keep running and some don't.

## 1:00 PM

The office is my house or Muthangari Drive, depending on the day, and Muthangari is about as good as an office gets in this city.

Part one had a commute, a desk, a building and fixed hours. Six years later the building is optional, and I still can't tell whether that's freedom or the loss of the last thing that made me leave the house.

## 4:00 PM

At 23 this was the Codecademy block: JSON from APIs, SQL queries, and a data science course I asked readers not to tell anyone about. I never finished it.

Now it's my own things. [Lock & Mercer](https://lockandmercer.com), the studio. SpaceYako. [godwillbarasa.com](https://godwillbarasa.com), where the work and the writing live.

At 23 I was learning things in case they became useful. At 28 I'm building things because nobody else is going to build them for me.

I noticed something while writing this. The front page of my site says most engineers hand a repository over and leave, and that I stay on the pager. Lock & Mercer's line is *we build it, then we carry it*, and the studio describes its markets as places where the hard part isn't the build, it's being believed. SpaceYako exists to tell you whether you can trust what someone has told you about a house.

Every one of those lines is a promise not to leave. I chose all of them, and until I sat down to write this I never noticed. I didn't understand why until later that night.

## 7:00 PM

At 23, seven o'clock was schoolwork, and sometimes classes.

Now it's therapy.

At 23 I wouldn't have written that sentence in public. I'm writing it because the most useful thing anyone did for me this year was sit in a room with me and ask better questions than I was asking myself.

I'm six sessions in. I went because a relationship ended.

Three things have come out of that room so far, and they're the parts I think generalise.

The first is that understanding is not the way out. I spent weeks reconstructing exactly what had happened, sure that if I could model it precisely enough it would stop hurting. It doesn't work like that. The reconstructing is the hurting, with better production values.

The second is that shame fixes nothing. More than once I asked to be spoken to harshly. It turns out self-disgust is what produces the bad decision at 1am, not what prevents it. What helps is friction and honesty. Contempt just teaches you to hide.

The third is that you can't outsource being witnessed. I write everything down: documents, logs, notes to myself, files for a future version of me. I thought that was organisation, and some of it is. Some of it is a man trying to be seen by a text file because he never asked an actual person.

## 9:00 PM

I've rewritten this part four times.

I found the love of my life. I lost her. I found her again. I lost her again.

That's all I'll say about her, because it's her life too and she didn't agree to be written about. But I'll tell you what I learned the second time, because it took me years and I'd have liked to know it sooner.

The first time, I ended it. I was fine. Sad, but fine. I functioned. I slept.

The second time, she ended it, and my world came apart.

Same woman, same relationship, every problem still sitting exactly where it had been. The only thing that changed was who did the leaving.

> The thing that nearly took me out was not losing her. It was being left.

Once I saw that, I saw it everywhere. Everything I do when I'm hurt is a version of leaving first. Delete the app. Delete the account. Quit cold turkey. Cut the person off. Burn it and walk. I've built a whole personality around never being the one left behind.

And then, at work, I built the exact opposite and called it positioning. The pager. The studio that carries what it builds. A product about whether you can believe what you're told.

> A man who cannot stand being left has built a career out of promising not to go.

It had to happen. I don't mean that the way people say it to make a bad year sound like a plan. I mean I had been trying to leave for months and couldn't, and something had to take the decision out of my hands before I would look at any of this.

## 10:30 PM

The other thing I think about at this hour is leaving the country.

It isn't a fringe thought here, and it isn't a disloyal one. In 2023 the government launched a labour export programme aiming to place a million Kenyans a year in jobs abroad, in Germany, Canada, the UK, the US, the UAE and Saudi Arabia. Last year Kenyans abroad sent home just over $5 billion. Remittances now bring in more foreign exchange than tea, coffee and horticulture combined.

This country's most valuable export is its people.

So a Kenyan engineer sitting up at half ten wondering whether to go isn't betraying the plan. He is the plan.

I still can't have the thought cleanly, because of everything I wrote an hour ago.

I'm a man whose response to pain is to leave first, sitting up at night wondering whether to leave an entire country. I honestly don't know how much of that is analysis and how much is the same reflex at national scale. From the inside they feel identical. That's the whole problem with a reflex.

So I've put the question down until I can pick it up without it being about her.

Ask me next year.

## 11:00 PM

At 23 I ended the day asleep by eleven, watching cartoons, ready to do it all again.

I'm not going to pretend to that. I don't know how tonight goes. Some nights I sleep now and some I don't, and I've stopped treating that as a verdict on whether I'm getting better.

What I have instead is a set of documents. I've been writing things down for the version of me who is going to lie about all of this in six months, because I know he's coming. He'll keep the good parts and lose the rest, so I wrote the rest down while I could still see it.

That's the difference between 23 and 28, I think. At 23 I was optimising the day. At 28 I'm trying to build something that survives the days I can't optimise.

The car was never the destination. Neither was the apartment, or the job, or the stack, or the morning routine.

I'll let you know what is when I find out.
